Is an Online "Friend" Grooming Your Child? Warning Signs & What To Do

If a child is in immediate danger, call 911 first. Everything else on this page can wait.

What grooming looks like

Grooming is when an adult — or an older teen — slowly builds trust with a child online so they can take advantage of that child. It often starts in a game, a group chat, or a social app, and it can look like friendship at first. Watch for a person who:

  • Gives your child special attention — constant messages, "you're my favorite," acting like they understand your child better than anyone.
  • Sends gifts — game currency, gift cards, in-game items, or presents, sometimes with strings attached.
  • Shower them with compliments — about their looks, their maturity, or how "grown up" they seem.
  • Asks for photos — first innocent ones, then more. Any adult asking a child for photos is a red flag, every time.
  • Asks your child to keep secrets — "this is just between us," "your parents wouldn't understand."
  • Moves the chat to a private app — pushing to leave the game or public app for a private or disappearing-message app.
  • Asks to meet in person — or asks where your child lives, goes to school, or will be and when.

Warning signs in the messages themselves

  • An adult or much older "friend" your child met only online.
  • Messages at odd hours, or a sudden flood of messages after your child met them.
  • "Don't tell your mom/dad." Secrecy is the biggest warning sign of all.
  • Questions about whether your child is home alone, or when parents are away.
  • Threats or guilt when your child pulls back: "After all I gave you…" or "I'll share your photos."
  • Your child hiding screens, deleting chats, or getting upset when you ask about an online friend.

What to do — step by step

  1. Stay calm. Your child needs to know they are not in trouble and it is not their fault. A scared child stops talking — and talking is how you protect them.
  2. Don't delete anything. Save screenshots of the messages, the person's profile, and usernames. That evidence is what investigators need.
  3. Don't confront the person. Don't message them back, don't warn them — they may delete accounts and evidence. Let law enforcement handle contact.
  4. Report it. Reports are free and you can make them any time:
    • NCMEC CyberTipline: report.cybertip.org or 1-800-843-5678 — the national reporting line for online child exploitation.
    • FBI: 1-800-CALL-FBI (1-800-225-5324).
    • Local police — your local department, or 911 if a child is in immediate danger.
  5. Block the person together with your child, after the screenshots are saved, and check the other apps and games your child uses for the same person.

A note for kids — please read this

Kids: If someone online asks you for photos, secrets, or to meet up — stop, don't reply, and tell a parent or a trusted adult right away. It is never your fault. Grown-ups who ask kids for those things are the ones doing wrong — never you. You can also report it free: report.cybertip.org or 1-800-843-5678 (CyberTipline).
