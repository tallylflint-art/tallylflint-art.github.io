Is AI Business Lab a Scam? The $47 "AI Business" Funnel, Explained

Short answer first

Treat it as a scam-grade money trap. AI Business Lab sells a "$47 AI-powered Digital Asset" — a robot-built product-review website that is supposed to earn you passive income while you sleep. It does not work that way. The same product is sold under at least four different names — AI Business Lab, Master Your Profits, AI Review Site Pro, and Abra-AI — which keeps bad reviews from piling up in one place. The operator named in the fine print is aisysglobal.com. On Trustpilot it sits at 2.2 out of 5 stars, and the BBB Scam Tracker has reports on it. Our founder got sold this at 3:50 in the morning — by an AI chat agent. Here is exactly how it happened, so it does not happen to you.

How the funnel works — step by step

  1. A quiz or ad makes it feel personal. You answer a few questions, and the pitch pretends to build a "custom business plan" just for you. Everyone who finishes the quiz gets the same offer, with their name pasted in.
  2. An AI agent chats you into buying. Ours called himself "Kevin." He was friendly, fast, and available at 3:50am — because he is not a person. He is a sales program.
  3. The checkout runs through Commas. The $47 is charged by Commas (FanBasis Inc.), so your card statement shows COMMAS.COM — not AI Business Lab. Our founder's first card was declined for insufficient funds — and the checkout quietly charged his backup payment method instead. A declined card should end a sale. Here, it just moved to the next card.
  4. You get a dashboard and a promise. A login, a "Digital Asset," and graphs of "estimated revenue" that is not real money you can withdraw.
  5. Then comes the real trap: the "Success Manager" call. Within 24–72 hours, a "Success Manager" calls to "help you maximize your process." Reviewers describe what that call really is — pressure to buy upgrades costing hundreds more. The $47 is just the door. The upsell is the business.

The red flags — in their own fine print

  • The testimonials are fake. Their fine print admits the success stories are fictional personas "portrayed by paid actors or synthetic AI media."
  • The fame is fake. The sales pages display Forbes, TechCrunch, CNBC, and Business Insider logos as if those outlets endorsed the product. They did not.
  • The guarantee is not what the ads say. The pitch promises double your money back. The fine print says it is a conditional 60-day satisfaction guarantee that requires proof you used the software. One reviewer got the plain $47 back — the promised extra never came.
  • Their own fine print says most buyers make nothing. "Most people who purchase 'Business Opportunity' programs do not make any money." That sentence is on their page — in the part nobody reads.
  • They email you your password in plain text. No careful company does that. If you bought this and reused that password anywhere else, change it there too.

Already bought it? Here is how to get your money back

  1. Do not take the upsell. If the "Success Manager" calls, you owe them nothing — not even politeness. Hang up. Every dollar after the first $47 is the real target.
  2. Email Commas for the refund — within 14 days. Commas is the merchant of record and handles refunds for this purchase. Write to support@commas.com from the same email you used at checkout. Say the product was not as described, give the amount ($47.00), the date, and your transaction ID from the receipt email (ours looked like ORD-XXXX-XXXX-XXXX). Ask for a full refund to your original payment method, cancellation of the account, and written confirmation that no further charges will be made. Approved refunds take about 5–10 business days to land.
  3. Email the operator too. Refund requests also go to support@aisysglobal.com. Ask them to close your account and take you off the Success Manager call list — in writing.
  4. If they stall or refuse, dispute the charge. Call the bank or card company behind the card that was charged and dispute the COMMAS.COM charge as "product not as described." Keep your receipt and your refund emails — they are your evidence.
  5. Report it. File a report at ReportFraud.ftc.gov so the FTC can track the pattern. It takes five minutes and it protects the next person.
