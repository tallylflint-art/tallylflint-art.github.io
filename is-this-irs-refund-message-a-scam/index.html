Is This IRS Refund Email or Text a Scam? How to Tell a Fake IRS Message

Short answer first

The IRS does not start a conversation about a refund or a tax debt by text, by email, or by a threatening phone call — and it never asks you to pay by gift card, wire transfer, or cryptocurrency. A message that does any of those things is not from the IRS, even if it uses the IRS name, a badge, or a case number.

How to spot the fake

A message is almost certainly a fake IRS message if it does one or more of these:

  • It arrives by text or email out of nowhere. A surprise refund notice or debt warning in your inbox or texts is the scammer's opening move, not how the IRS first reaches you.
  • It threatens you. "Pay now or police will arrest you," "your account will be frozen today," "a warrant is out." Threats are meant to stop you thinking. A real tax office sends letters and gives you time and a way to appeal.
  • It demands an unusual payment. Gift cards, wire transfers, crypto, or a payment app link in the message itself. Real tax payments do not work that way.
  • It asks you to click to claim a refund. A link that promises a fast refund if you enter your bank details or Social Security number is after your identity, not your refund.
  • It rushes you past checking. "Do this in the next hour," "do not tell anyone," "do not hang up." Any message that forbids you from checking is telling you why you should check.

Why this lure works so well: the Oct 25, 2026 AntiScam™ research puts impersonation as the most-reported FTC category — more than 1 million reports and more than $3.5 billion in FTC 2025 figures — and the FBI's Sept 17, 2026 warning counted about 61,000 government / law-enforcement impersonation complaints and more than $1.6 billion from Jan 2025 to July 2026. Government names carry weight, and scammers borrow that weight.

What to do right now

  1. Hang up, or stop reading, and do nothing the message asks. Do not pay, do not tap the link, do not call the number in the message.
  2. Do not hand over personal information — no Social Security number, no bank login, no ID photo, no matter what the message claims will happen.
  3. Check separately, on your own. If you are worried about a real tax matter, go to the IRS website yourself or call a number you look up yourself — never the one the message gave you.
  4. Keep the message, then delete it from your inbox flow. A screenshot helps if you report it.
  5. Report it. Forward a scam text to 7726 and report it at ReportFraud.ftc.gov. If you already sent money, contact your bank or card company at once and say it was a scam.
