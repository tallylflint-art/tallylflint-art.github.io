Is This Bank Account Locked Text a Scam? How to Tell a Fake Bank Message

Short answer first

A real bank will never text or email you a link and ask you to log in through it to unlock your account, and it will never ask you to move your money to a "safe account." If a message does either of those things, it is a scam — even if it shows your bank's name and logo, because logos are easy to copy.

How to tell the bank message is fake

Be suspicious fast if you see any of these:

  • A login link in the message. "Verify now," "unlock here," "confirm your identity" — all pointing to a page that looks like your bank but is not. Real banks tell you to open their own app or type their own address yourself.
  • A "safe account" instruction. "Move your money to this safe account while we investigate." There is no safe account. Money moved there goes to the scammer.
  • A request for codes or full passwords. A one-time code sent to your phone, your full password, or your PIN. Your bank already has those systems — it does not ask you to read them back to a stranger.
  • Panic wording. "Your account will be closed in 2 hours," "unusual activity detected, act now," "your money is at risk." Panic is the tool. A real fraud team freezes a card and lets you call back calmly.
  • A callback number that is not your bank's. The message gives you a number to ring. That number reaches the scammer, not your bank.

The Oct 25, 2026 AntiScam™ research lists bank "safe account" messages as a live lure in a Missouri guide verified Oct 7, 2026, and puts phone and website/app losses at $1.1 billion each in FTC 2025 contact-method figures (via secondary summary). This is the lure where one tap can empty an account, which is why the rule below matters most: never log in from a message link.

What to do right now

  1. Do not tap the link and do not log in through it. Close the message. Do not enter a username, password, or code anywhere it points.
  2. Do not move money anywhere the message suggests, and do not read a code back to a caller who rang about the message.
  3. Contact your bank yourself. Open your bank's own app, or ring the number on the back of your card or on your bank statement — not the number in the message — and ask: "Is there really a lock or suspicious activity on my account?"
  4. If you already tapped and logged in, ring your bank at once, tell them it was a scam login, and ask them to freeze or reset access. Change that password from your bank's own app or site, not from any link.
  5. Report it. Forward a scam text to 7726 and report it at ReportFraud.ftc.gov.
