Is This Tech Support Call a Scam? How to Tell

Short answer first

Almost certainly yes. Microsoft, Apple, and Geek Squad never call you out of the blue about a virus — not ever. And a pop-up that freezes your screen, blares a warning, and shows a phone number to call is fake, every time. Real companies do not watch your computer, do not lock your screen, and do not ask you to call a number on a warning. Never call the number. Never let a stranger take control of your computer.

How to tell it is a scam

  • They contacted you first. A call "from Microsoft" or "from Apple" warning about a virus is a scammer with a script. Real tech companies wait for you to contact them — they never phone you about a problem on your computer.
  • A pop-up locks your screen and shows a phone number. The page may flash, beep, or talk in a robot voice: "Your computer is infected — call support now." That is a web page pretending to be a warning. The number rings the scammer, not Microsoft.
  • They want remote access to your computer. They will ask you to install AnyDesk, TeamViewer, or a similar program so they can "fix it." Once inside, they can steal passwords, bank details, and personal files — and plant real problems to charge you for.
  • They show you "proof" that means nothing. They open normal system screens full of ordinary warnings and call them viruses, or type commands that print scary-looking text. Every healthy computer has those same screens.
  • The fee must be paid in gift cards, wire transfer, or crypto. Real support is paid by card on the company's real website. Gift cards are the scammer's favorite because the money is gone the moment you read out the numbers.
  • They rush and frighten you. "Hackers are draining your bank account right now." Fear and hurry are the whole trick — they need you to act before you think.

What to do right now

  1. If a pop-up has locked your screen: do not call the number. Close the browser tab or window. If it will not close, hold the power button to shut the computer down, wait a minute, and turn it back on. The "lock" is just a web page — restarting clears it.
  2. If you are on a call like this, hang up. You do not owe a stranger politeness, and no real company will be offended.
  3. Never give remote access — if you already let them in, disconnect from the internet right away (unplug the cable or turn off Wi-Fi) and shut the computer down. Have a local shop you chose yourself, or your computer maker's real support line, check it before you use it for banking again.
  4. Run your own antivirus scan — the one already on your computer, opened by you, not by a link or a caller.
  5. If you paid, call your bank right now at the number on the back of your card. Tell them it was a tech-support scam and ask them to stop the payment and watch your accounts. If you paid by gift card, call the gift card company immediately — fast action sometimes recovers the money.
  6. Change your important passwords from a clean device (a phone or a family member's computer) if the scammer had access to your machine — email and bank passwords first.
  7. Report it. Report the scam at ReportFraud.ftc.gov and to the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center at ic3.gov. You can also tell your local police department's non-emergency line — your report helps protect the next person.
