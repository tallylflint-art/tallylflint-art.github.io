Is This Bank Alert a Scam? How to Tell

Short answer first

It might be — and the safe move is always the same: never tap the link in the message, and never call the number in the message. Scammers copy real bank fraud alerts almost perfectly, right down to the logo. Your bank will never ask for your PIN, your full card number, or a security code by text. If an "alert" asks for any of that — or pressures you to act in the next few minutes — it is a scam.

How to tell it is a scam

  • It has a link to tap or a number to call. That link leads to a fake bank page built to steal your login. That number rings the scammer, not your bank — even when the caller ID shows your bank's real name and number, because those can be faked (spoofed).
  • The "reply YES or NO" trap. A text asks, "Did you make this charge? Reply YES or NO." If you answer NO, a "bank agent" calls you right away and walks you into handing over your money. A reply of any kind tells the scammer your number is live and you are worried — exactly what they want.
  • It asks for your PIN, full card number, or a code. Your bank never asks for these by text, email, or an incoming call. Anyone who asks is not your bank.
  • It tells you to move your money. "Transfer your money to a safe account," "withdraw cash and deposit it elsewhere," "buy gift cards to protect your account." Real banks never say this. There is no "safe account" — there is only the scammer's account.
  • It rushes you. "Your account will be closed," "a $900 charge is going through now." Real fraud departments can freeze a card without your help and without a countdown.
  • A caller asks you to keep it secret. "Do not tell the bank staff, they are part of it." That line exists to cut you off from the very people who would stop the scam.

What to do right now

  1. Stop. Do not tap the link, do not reply, and do not call the number in the message. If you are on a call like this, hang up — you can always reach your real bank yourself.
  2. Call the number on the back of your card. That number is printed by your bank and cannot be faked by a text. Ask them directly: "Is there a problem with my account?"
  3. Or open your bank's app or website yourself — type the address in, never through a link from the message — and look at your real account.
  4. Never share a PIN, full card number, or one-time code with anyone who contacted you first. Your bank already has your information; it does not need you to read it back.
  5. Report it. Forward a scam text to 7726 (that spells SPAM on most phones), and report the scam at ReportFraud.ftc.gov. If you already sent money or gave out details, call your bank at the number on your card right away and tell them what happened — acting fast gives you the best chance of getting money back.
