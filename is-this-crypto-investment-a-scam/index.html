Is This Crypto Investment a Scam? How to Tell

Short answer first

If the returns are "guaranteed," it is a lie — no real investment can promise big, fast, safe profits. And if a new online friend — someone you have never met in person — is steering you toward a crypto trading platform, that is the classic setup for one of the costliest scams running today, often called "pig butchering." The profits on the screen are fake. The only real money in it is yours, going out.

How to tell it is a scam

  • Guaranteed high returns with little or no risk. That combination does not exist in real investing. Anyone who promises it is selling you a story, not an investment.
  • A new online friend brings up investing. It starts friendly — a dating app, a social media message, even a "wrong number" text that turns into daily chats. Sooner or later your new friend mentions crypto, shows off their own "winnings," and offers to teach you. That friendship is the bait, and it was planned from the first message.
  • You are sent to an unfamiliar platform or app. The site looks polished and your balance climbs fast — but the numbers are typed in by the scammer. Real, well-known exchanges do not need a new friend to recruit you by chat.
  • Small withdrawals work at first. The platform may let you take out a little money early on. That is deliberate — it builds your trust so you will put in much more.
  • Then come the fees. When you try to withdraw the big amount, suddenly there is a "tax," a "verification fee," or an "unlock charge" you must pay first — then another, and another. Real platforms take fees out of your balance; they never demand fresh money before releasing yours.
  • Pressure and secrecy. "This window closes tonight." "Do not tell your bank what it is for." Real investments do not expire in hours, and no honest opportunity needs you to hide it from your bank or family.

What to do right now

  1. Stop sending money — all of it, today. No more deposits, no "fees," no top-ups to "unlock" your account. Every extra dollar goes to the same scammers. Paying a fee never frees your money; it only loses more.
  2. Do not let them rush or guilt you. Your "friend" may turn cold, angry, or tearful when you stop. That is part of the script. Block and walk away — you owe them nothing.
  3. Save everything. Keep the chats, the platform web address, wallet addresses, receipts, and bank records. Do not delete the conversation — it is evidence, and it helps investigators and your bank.
  4. Tell your bank right away at the number on the back of your card. Tell them it was a crypto investment scam and ask what can be stopped or recalled. Be honest about what the money was for — bank staff have seen this many times and fast action gives you the best chance.
  5. Know the hard truth: once crypto is sent, it is almost never recoverable — and anyone who contacts you promising to get it back for a fee is running the same scam a second time. Never pay a "recovery agent."
  6. Report it. Report the scam at ReportFraud.ftc.gov and file a report with the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center at ic3.gov — IC3 tracks exactly these cases. You can also report it to your local police department. Reporting is how these networks get shut down.
  7. Tell someone you trust. These scams are built by professionals and fool smart people every day — there is no shame in it. Telling family keeps them from being targeted next.
