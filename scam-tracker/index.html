Confirmed scam numbers — on our shared warning list

These numbers are confirmed scams — real people got these texts, our team verified them, and every one is on the shared warning list our free checker uses. If any of these numbers calls or texts you: do not reply, do not tap anything, just delete it.

  • +1 (833) 940-9354 — Fake $2,792.50 insurance refund text. It claims you have an insurance refund waiting and asks you to confirm to claim it. There is no refund — confirming is how they hook you.
  • +1 (833) 991-6285 — Fake $800 "Daylendzone" loan text. It dangles an $800 loan and asks you to verify to proceed. There is no loan — it's bait to steal your details.
  • +1 (844) 906-4483 — Fake $1,200 "NestNegotiate" loan text. It claims a loan was already accepted in your name. It wasn't — it's pressure bait to get you to call back and hand over information.

Heads up: scammers change numbers constantly, and a real-looking number can still be faked ("spoofed"). A strange number isn't automatically a scam — but any number asking you to confirm, verify, or claim money you weren't expecting should be checked before you answer. The scam tricks hitting hardest this week

  • AI Business Lab — the $47 "AI business" funnel
    NEW this week, and it hit home: an AI chat agent sold our founder a $47 "AI-powered business" at 3:50 in the morning, and when his first card was declined it quietly charged his backup. Same product sells as Master Your Profits, AI Review Site Pro, and Abra-AI. The $47 is just the door — a "Success Manager" upsell call follows. Full breakdown and refund steps on the page.
  • Medicare & Social Security messages
    Calls, texts, and emails claiming your benefits are in trouble. Medicare and Social Security do not contact you like that — and they never threaten to stop your benefits by phone.
  • Fake job offers
    A "job" that finds you by text, pays a lot for simple tasks, and sends you a check to shop with is a scam — employment scams were the #2 riskiest scam of 2025.
  • QR code scams
    A QR code is a hidden web address. If it's a sticker slapped over another code, or the page it opens asks for payment or a login — stop.

Protect your kids — this week's family warning

Parents: grooming often starts as a friendly message in a game or app — special attention, gifts, compliments, then requests for photos, secrets, or a meet-up. Talk to your kids: if a stranger online asks for photos, secrets, gifts, or to meet up, STOP, don't reply, and tell a parent or trusted adult right away. Save the messages, don't delete them, and report it free: report.cybertip.org (NCMEC CyberTipline) or 1-800-843-5678 — FBI: 1-800-CALL-FBI — or your local police. Full warning signs and step-by-step help: Protect Kids Online.
